Case 01 · Positioning & packaging

Selling infrastructure monitoring to a bank that did not know it could be bought

A pan-African bank running around 600 branches had no central view of its own infrastructure. The obstacle was never price. It was that nobody in the room believed this was a thing you could purchase.

Input · what the product sheet said

iPulse. SaaS-based IoT monitoring and analytics. Polls power, generator runtime, UPS health, battery backup, cooling and fuel telemetry from remote sites into a single analytics layer.

Output · what the buyer actually bought

You will know a branch is going down before your customers tell you about it in the morning. And you will pay for it monthly, not buy it.

The situation

iPulse is an IoT product for infrastructure monitoring. It is genuinely useful and it was genuinely hard to sell, because everything true about it was written in the language of the people who built it rather than the people who buy it.

The first customer I worked in front of was a bank operating around 600 branches across several African countries. Nothing at branch level was centrally visible. Power failures, generator usage, UPS health, battery backup, cooling systems and fuel consumption were all managed locally, branch by branch. The consequences were specific:

  • Most issues were only discovered after a branch had already gone down.
  • Fuel theft could not be tracked properly.
  • Maintenance was reactive rather than preventive.
  • There was no single dashboard for any of it.

Underneath that sat the real operating problem: a different vendor for every layer. When the network or an ATM failed overnight, the bank usually heard about it from its own customers the next morning. Then it called the vendor. Then it waited three to seven days for a resolution, losing business the entire time.

The customer wanted this. They did not know it existed, and on the first call they did not believe it was possible.

That disbelief was the actual sales obstacle, and it shaped everything I built afterwards. The objection was not price. It was plausibility, followed immediately by a second objection: how could one provider possibly manage this many branches and ATMs.

What I owned

Product marketing and go-to-market for iPulse, iNaaS and the satellite connectivity portfolio. In practice that meant two jobs in sequence. First, understand the product properly and understand what the customer was actually losing money on. Second, translate a very technical solution into simple business language, without hollowing out the substance.

  • Complete product positioning, messaging and the sales story.
  • Buyer personas and segment-level value propositions for banking, retail and telecom.
  • Go-to-market strategy and launch plans.
  • The commercial structure: service models, packaging and the expansion path.

What I built

The sales team needed to hold the whole story without me in the room, so the collateral system was built end to end rather than as one-off assets.

  • Industry brochures, one-pagers, sales decks and proposal templates.
  • Four enterprise case studies: banking 5G, MNO tower infrastructure, logistics and NaaS.
  • Proposal and quote templates built into Zoho CRM, so quoting became a process rather than a document hunt.
  • Website content, AI explainer videos and the social content engine.
  • The marketing operations layer: deliverables tracker, pipeline reporting and a monthly reporting cadence to the CEO.

The commercial model

The instinct in the room was to sell a single package. I recommended three service models instead, because the barrier was belief rather than budget, and a single package forces a customer to make one large decision about something they are not yet sure works.

The recommendation was to open with 100 branches under a fully managed OPEX model on a five-year contract, then expand across the remaining branches once the first phase demonstrated results. Managed services and OPEX did more work than any brochure. It answered the second objection directly: the bank was not being asked to run this, and it was not being asked to buy an asset.

Where it landed

  • The bank started with 100 branches on a five-year contract, and is now assessing performance before releasing further branches.
  • Across the portfolio, $256K+ in deals closed to date on products that had no positioning, packaging or sales collateral when I arrived.
  • Both iPulse and iNaaS moved from unpositioned to packaged, revenue-generating offerings.
  • Partnership and ecosystem-led growth across Middle East and Sub-Saharan African markets, working directly with C-suite stakeholders.

Contract values for the end customer are withheld. The portfolio figure is the one I report on.

What I would carry into the next one

When a category is unfamiliar to the buyer, the first sale is not a pricing negotiation, it is a plausibility problem. Packaging is the argument. Three service models, a managed OPEX structure and a first phase small enough to approve did more to close that account than any single piece of messaging.

Case 02 · Growth & creative strategy

Making an 88-year-old the face of a D2C brand, and proving it with data

In a category full of near-identical brands, the only asset competitors could not copy was a person. The rest was disciplined testing.

  • Employer
    The Teaser Company
  • Role
    Senior Manager, Account Growth & GTM Strategy
  • Brand
    Early-stage D2C food & coffee brand. Name withheld.
  • Period
    Nov 2023 to Mar 2025
  • Market
    India, expanding beyond the South
6x to 9.5xROAS on winning campaigns, identified inside the first month
Higher AOVfrom milestone-gated cart rewards, not from discounting
$300K → $450KARR across the wider account book, up 50% in 18 months
Input · how the category saw it

Another direct-to-consumer snack and coffee brand, in a category already full of direct-to-consumer snack and coffee brands, with awareness concentrated in one region.

Output · how we sold it

An 88-year-old who has been making this by hand for more than 40 years, and now wants the rest of India to taste it.

The situation

The brand had a good product and a founding team stuck on direction. There were many brands in the same space, and no clear answer to why a buyer outside the region should pick this one.

  • Awareness was low outside South India.
  • Customer trust was still being built.
  • They needed to acquire customers profitably, not simply by spending more.

The challenge was never running Meta ads. It was building a repeatable growth engine, so that a winning campaign was the start of a system rather than a lucky month.

What I owned

The complete growth strategy: customer journey, creative strategy, Meta campaigns, audience testing, reporting and scaling.

The bet

The strongest asset the brand had was not a product claim. It was the founder's 88-year-old grandmother, who had been making these recipes for more than 40 years. We made her the face of the brand, on the view that people would connect with authenticity faster than with polished influencer content.

The team travelled from Delhi to Chennai to shoot with her. In her own words, she explained what makes hers different from everything else on the shelf, that she has been making it with care for more than four decades, and that she now wants to take it beyond South India properly rather than accidentally.

The traditional formats did not work. What worked was UGC, emotional connect, breaking-news style and podcast style.

How we tested

Nothing was decided on opinion. Every format was tested and judged on campaign data: ROAS, CTR, CPA, CPC, retention and engagement. We ran UGC, recipe videos, product demos and lifestyle content, then scaled only the creatives that actually performed and cut the rest quickly.

Fixing the checkout, not just the ad

Traffic was only half the problem. People were dropping off at the landing page, so we gamified the cart: goodies and freebies attached to price milestones, plus gift boxes. It was a joint move between creative and strategy rather than a discount lever, and it lifted average order value and, with it, purchases.

Where it landed

  • Multiple winning creatives identified within the first month.
  • Some campaigns delivered between 6x and 9.5x ROAS.
  • Acquisition costs came down and overall campaign efficiency improved.
  • Average order value increased through the cart and milestone work.

The more useful outcome was not the campaign numbers. It was a repeatable creative testing framework and a growth playbook the team kept using as the brand expanded into marketplaces, quick commerce and retail. The same way of working was then applied across other brands in the portfolio I managed.

The wider portfolio

Across the accounts I owned at The Teaser Company, the enterprise portfolio grew from $300K to $450K ARR, up 50% in 18 months, across 10+ key accounts, with account profitability improved by about 20% through structured upsell and cross-sell.

What I would carry into the next one

In a crowded category, positioning is usually a person, not an adjective. The grandmother was not a creative gimmick, she was the only thing competitors could not copy. Everything after that was disciplined testing, and the playbook mattered more than any single winning ad.

Case 03 · Market entry & partnerships

Entering the UAE from India, with a pipeline to show for it

Entering a market from outside it is a trust problem before it is a pipeline problem. Partners, spec work and a small paid pilot all do the same job: they let a buyer test you cheaply.

  • Client
    Digidarts, performance marketing agency
  • Period
    Mar 2025 to Jun 2025
  • Markets
    UAE and wider MENA, North America, APAC
  • Mandate
    International GTM and market entry
  • Landed
    A regional super app and a UAE bank. Names withheld.

1 systempartner map, outbound engine and playbooks the company retained afterwards
Input · where it started

A well-regarded Indian agency with international ambition and ad hoc business development. Occasional conversations, no market map, no repeatable process.

Output · where it ended

A named list of the accounts, partners and decision makers worth calling in the UAE, and a system for calling them that keeps running after the consultant leaves.

The situation

The company already had a good reputation in India, but there was no proper plan for international expansion. Most business development was happening in an ad hoc way. The brief I set was to build a growth engine rather than to find a few clients, because finding a few clients solves one quarter and a system solves the market.

The market map

I started with research, and made it specific enough to act on:

  • Which countries actually made sense to enter first, and which industries to target.
  • What the open briefs in the UAE market were at that moment.
  • Which partners were already operating in the market and worth working with.
  • Which Shopify companies operating in the UAE could become partners rather than prospects.
  • Who the real decision makers were, by name.

From that I built the go-to-market strategy, defined the ICPs, shortlisted target accounts and put together a revenue plan, so that every activity mapped back to a business goal rather than to activity for its own sake. The deliverables were operational: contact data for consultants, LinkedIn outreach automation running through AI agents, contacts for Shopify app partners we could work with on a client-by-client basis, and a list of UAE agencies with multi-country clients where the relationship could run both ways. They could deliver for us in the UAE and we could deliver for them in India.

Partnerships as the primary channel

A large part of the work was partner-led rather than direct. I researched Shopify partners, SaaS companies, AI platforms, consultants and agencies across the UAE and MENA that could become referral or co-selling partners, on the view that an unknown agency entering a new market borrows trust faster than it builds it.

The outbound system

Alongside that I built the outbound process end to end: LinkedIn outreach, email sequences, WhatsApp follow-ups, discovery calls, case studies, proposals, pilot projects, and the conversion of those pilots into long-term retainers. To make it repeatable I also built the sales playbooks, case studies, outreach templates, meeting trackers, pipeline reports and weekly execution plans, so the team was not starting from a blank page every week.

The goal was never lead generation. It was a repeatable international business development model the company could keep using in the next market.

Two worked examples

A regional super app

We had been tracking a head of growth at a large Indian quick-commerce brand. He moved to the super app as head of marketing, and that relationship gave us the meeting. We proposed starting with a pilot and pitched several products: app store optimisation for downloads, AI search visibility so that the brand surfaces when someone asks an AI assistant which company is best in Dubai, plus SEO and performance marketing. Performance marketing and AI search visibility were what landed. It closed on a monthly retainer with managed spend.

A UAE bank

Once the first account was cracked we moved immediately, while we had proof. I found the contact through LinkedIn automation, started with a marketing executive, and he introduced the head of marketing. We could not get a slot, so instead of chasing it we sent examples of our work, and then, with Eid approaching, built spec work for the bank rather than asking for more time. That converted into a pilot on a single service, creatives plus performance, running around 50 days and structured to move onto a retainer afterwards. We also introduced partners with a global presence and supported website optimisation work.

The commercial model

Revenue was a retainer plus a percentage of managed media spend. As the account grows, through higher ROAS, more purchase orders, more impressions, lower CAC and higher AOV, the spend grows and the agency's revenue grows with it. That structure aligns the agency to the client's performance rather than to the invoice, and it is the reason pilots were priced to convert rather than to maximise the first cheque.

Where it landed

  • Two flagship UAE accounts onboarded inside the engagement, a regional super app and a bank, both starting as pilots structured to become retainers.
  • Deals in the $20K to $150K+ range closed and managed across UAE, KSA, Qatar, Kuwait, Bahrain and North America.
  • A market-entry model, partner map and outbound system the company retained after the engagement ended.

The two accounts above are the most recent examples. Other engagements in other markets are not described here.

What I would carry into the next one

The spec work built around Eid closed a bank that would not give us a meeting. That is a better argument for doing the work upfront than any follow-up sequence I could write.
